Milling vs 3D Printing in Digital Denture Fabrication

A literature-based exploration of mechanical performance, long-term durability, repairability, and workflow efficiency.

Overview

Abstract

Digital denture fabrication increasingly uses subtractive manufacturing (milling) and additive manufacturing (3D printing). Both approaches can support digital workflows, but their performance depends on material composition, manufacturing parameters, post-processing, prosthesis design, and clinical protocols.

This review explores the mechanical properties, durability, repairability, and workflow efficiency of milled and 3D-printed denture base materials. It also identifies questions that require further investigation using peer-reviewed evidence.

Keywords: digital dentures, CAD/CAM, milling, 3D printing, PMMA, flexural strength, fatigue resistance, relining, workflow efficiency.

01 / Context

Background

Digital denture workflows may include digital impressions or laboratory scanning, computer-aided design (CAD), and manufacturing through milling or 3D printing. These approaches differ in how the prosthesis is manufactured, processed, and finished.

Milling is a subtractive process in which a restoration is machined from a pre-manufactured blank. Three-dimensional printing is an additive process in which material is built layer by layer. Neither label alone describes every material system or clinical outcome.

My experience with both manufacturing methods has led me to focus on a practical question: how can a dental laboratory achieve consistent quality and accuracy while maintaining reliable long-term performance and an efficient production workflow?

Central research question How do milled and 3D-printed denture base materials compare in mechanical strength, long-term durability, repairability, and total production efficiency?
02 / Material Performance

Mechanical Properties and Durability

2.1 Milled denture bases

Milled denture bases are commonly produced from pre-polymerised resin blanks. Their performance may depend on material composition, blank manufacturing, machining strategy, tool condition, design geometry, and finishing.

2.2 3D-printed denture bases

Printed denture bases are manufactured using validated printable materials and a suitable additive process. Print orientation, layer settings, washing, post-curing, and material ageing can influence the properties of the final product.

2.3 Properties that should be compared

Property Why it matters Evidence to examine
Flexural strength Resistance to bending under load. Comparable specimen geometry and test methods.
Fracture resistance Resistance to cracking or catastrophic failure. Fracture tests and failure modes.
Fatigue resistance Performance under repeated loading. Cyclic loading and ageing studies.
Dimensional accuracy Reproducibility of shape and fit. Validated measurement and clinical relevance.
Ageing stability Changes after environmental exposure. Water storage, thermocycling, and longer-term studies.

A fair comparison should distinguish the manufacturing method from the specific material. Results for one milled resin or one printed resin cannot automatically be applied to every product in that category.

03 / Literature Review

Evidence from the Literature

3.1 Flexural strength

Abualsaud and Gad (2022) published a systematic review and meta-analysis of in-vitro studies evaluating the flexural strength of CAD/CAM denture base materials. Their reported pooled mean values were 120.61 MPa for milled PMMA and 92.16 MPa for heat-polymerised PMMA.

Material group Reported pooled mean flexural strength
Milled PMMA 120.61 MPa
Heat-polymerised PMMA 92.16 MPa

These findings suggest that the milled PMMA materials included in the analysis had higher pooled flexural strength than conventional heat-polymerised PMMA. This is not, however, a universal direct comparison between all milled and 3D-printed materials.

Read the 2022 systematic review

3.2 Reline and denture tooth bonding

A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis by Arzani and colleagues examined bonding strength between denture base materials and reline materials or denture teeth in milled and 3D-printed complete dentures. The authors reported results that generally favoured milled bases for reline bonding.

The authors also reported that tooth-base bond strength favoured milled bases after a sensitivity analysis excluded two outlier comparisons. This qualification matters because results can be affected by substantial differences among the included studies.

Read the 2025 systematic review

3.3 Summary of current evidence

Outcome Current finding Important limitation
Flexural strength Milled PMMA had a higher pooled mean than heat-polymerised PMMA in the 2022 review. This does not establish superiority over every printed resin.
Reline bonding The 2025 review generally favoured milled bases. Laboratory bond strength is not the same as clinical longevity.
Tooth bonding Results favoured milled bases after sensitivity analysis. Study heterogeneity and outlier handling affect interpretation.
Long-term durability Further targeted investigation is needed. Laboratory findings alone cannot determine long-term clinical survival.

3.4 Preliminary interpretation

The available evidence suggests potential advantages for certain milled denture base materials in flexural strength and bonding performance. However, the findings do not establish that milling is always superior. Material selection, processing protocols, ageing, design, and clinical conditions must also be considered.

Evidence note: The numerical results above are reported findings from the cited reviews, not results from experiments conducted as part of this project.

04 / Clinical Relevance

Relining, Rebasing, and Repairability

A practical concern is whether a 3D-printed denture can be reliably relined or rebased when its fit changes. The answer depends on the specific denture material, the repair resin, surface preparation, and the validated clinical protocol.

  • Identify the denture base material and its intended use.
  • Check manufacturer instructions for repair and relining.
  • Examine the effects of surface treatment and bonding agents.
  • Compare bond strength before and after ageing.
  • Distinguish laboratory bond tests from clinical repair outcomes.

Further research should evaluate whether differences in laboratory bond strength lead to clinically meaningful differences in repair success, maintenance requirements, and long-term patient outcomes.

05 / Laboratory Practice

Workflow and Production Efficiency

Manufacturing time alone is not an adequate measure of production efficiency. A useful comparison should consider the complete process, from case preparation to final quality control and delivery.

Workflow factor Questions to investigate
Design and setup How much technician time is required before manufacturing?
Machine time How long does each method take for comparable cases?
Post-processing What washing, curing, finishing, or polishing is required?
Labour How much active technician time is needed?
Quality control What checks are needed to confirm fit and accuracy?
Cost What are the material, maintenance, equipment, and rework costs?

A fair comparison should use similar case types and report total turnaround time, active labour, material consumption, rework, and overall cost. The most efficient option may vary by laboratory volume, case complexity, equipment, and material system.

06 / Personal Research Agenda

Research Questions and Reflection

  1. How do flexural strength, fracture resistance, and fatigue behaviour compare between specific milled and 3D-printed denture base materials?
  2. How do water ageing and thermocycling affect their long-term mechanical properties?
  3. What evidence supports the long-term reliability of relining, rebasing, and repair protocols for printed denture bases?
  4. How do milling tool wear and machining strategies influence surface quality, dimensional accuracy, and material integrity?
  5. When clinical outcomes are comparable, could patients be offered a choice based on cost, turnaround time, repairability, and clinician recommendations?
  6. Which workflow offers the best balance of quality, production time, and cost for different denture cases?

Working position

My primary priorities are quality and accuracy. I do not assume that either manufacturing method is universally superior. My aim is to evaluate specific material systems using published evidence and consider how the findings apply to everyday dental laboratory practice.

07 / Research Process

Proposed Literature Review Method

A structured search should be conducted using databases such as PubMed and relevant dental materials journals. Search terms may include:

  • "3D printed denture base" AND "milled denture base"
  • "flexural strength" AND denture base materials
  • "fatigue resistance" AND digital dentures
  • "reline bond strength" AND 3D printing
  • "water ageing" AND denture base resin
  • "digital denture workflow" AND efficiency

For each included study, record the authors, publication year, material and manufacturer, specimen preparation, post-processing, test method, ageing conditions, findings, and study limitations.

Numerical results from different studies should not be directly combined when the materials, testing methods, or experimental conditions differ substantially. The review should distinguish laboratory evidence from clinical evidence.
